**Teller A – Deployment:**  
November 2, 1988. 8:30 p.m.  
From an MIT computer, a 23-year-old Cornell student named Robert Morris releases a worm.  

Unlike a virus, a worm needs no host. It spreads on its own.  

It targets Unix systems. It hides itself. It uses a backdoor in email and a bug in the "finger" program.  

Morris intended it to spread slowly and secretly. But a programming error made it replicate out of control.  

Within 24 hours, 6,000 computers are infected. Only 60,000 existed online.  

No files destroyed. But systems slow to a halt. Emails delayed for days. Damage estimates reach millions.

**Teller B – Aftermath:**  
Morris feels guilt. He asks a friend to send an anonymous apology and removal instructions online. The network is so damaged that almost no one receives it.  

Another friend calls The New York Times. Accidentally gives Morris’s initials: RTM. The Times identifies him.  

The FBI investigates. Decrypts his files. Finds clear evidence.  

Morris is convicted under the 1986 Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. First person ever. Sentence: fine, probation, 400 hours of community service. No jail time.  

Days after the attack, the Department of Defense creates the first computer emergency response team. Intrusion detection software follows. 

But the Morris Worm also inspires a new generation of hackers. Internet attacks continue today.  

The first Internet attack. A wake-up call the hard way.
